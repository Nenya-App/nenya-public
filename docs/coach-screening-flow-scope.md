# Scope: live screening of coach messages with a small model, and how it improves without user data

Written 2026-11-12. Nothing here is built. It extends `docs/coach-detection-rescope-ethos.md` (which kept the matcher on the server and scoped the model for the witnessed side only) to **live screening**, at the owner's request. Sizes, limits and licences marked (verify) are from memory.

## 1. What we need (and do not)
- **A second signal beside the phrase matcher**, to catch what words miss (implicit contempt, dehumanizing comparisons without a listed word) and to avoid what words get wrong (quotes, grief, reclaimed words, which the model reads in context better than a list).
- **Gentle outcomes only from the model alone**: offer a card, soften or turn the reply toward feelings and needs. **A boundary or an ending needs the matcher and the model to agree, or the existing harm-to-others rule.** One signal never ends a conversation.
- **Nothing stored.** No message, score or identifier is kept by the checks. The ladder's state lives in the person's browser for the visit, as designed.
- **Honesty about it.** People can see that the checks exist, how they work and what they can do; they can contest a decision. They cannot switch the checks off (the safety purpose), and the text says why.
- **Improvement without real-user text.**

## 2. The stack
**The flow, per message, on the server (in memory only):**
1. Existing guards first (crisis and harm-to-others stops, the AI-directed-abuse path): unchanged and unaffected by the model.
2. The matcher (private list, cue words, the neutral-identity guard, reporting-frame rule) runs: microseconds.
3. The model scores the **latest message** (not the history) **in parallel with the call to Venice**, so it adds no waiting. If it has not answered within a short budget (for example 400 ms), the reply goes on **without it** (fail open to the matcher), and an aggregate counter ticks `model_unavailable`.
4. A small decision table (section 3) combines the two into one of: nothing, offer a card, soften, boundary level 1 or 2. The reply and the transcript note record **which checker said what, by rule name**, never the score.
5. Memory is released. Nothing is logged except aggregate counters (section 5).

**The model.**
- **Candidates:** a ToxiGen-trained RoBERTa (about 125M parameters, int8 ONNX about 125 MB (verify)) is the obvious one; a distilled model or a MiniLM-sized classifier (about 20 to 40 MB) is better for a function. **First step is a bake-off on our evaluation sets (section 4), not a choice by reputation.** Check each model card's licence and training data (verify) before use.
- **Runtime:** `onnxruntime-node` (or transformers.js, which wraps it), int8 quantized, CPU, single short input: about tens to low hundreds of milliseconds warm.
- **Where it lives.** Either **bundled in the function** (if the unzipped bundle stays under the host's limit, about 250 MB (verify)), or **in Netlify Blobs** and fetched on a cold start into the function's temporary disk, then reused while the instance is warm. Bundled is simpler and faster at cold start; Blobs lets the model change without a deploy and keeps a large file out of the build. **Recommendation: bundle a small distilled model; use Blobs only if the model is too large.**
- **A separate small function** (`screen`) called by the relay, or the same function with lazy loading? A separate one keeps the relay's cold start small and lets the screening fail without taking the coach down. Our own function calling our own function keeps the text on our infrastructure. **Recommendation: separate function.**
- **No third party sees the text for screening.** This stays true only if the model runs in our function; it is the reason for not using a hosted moderation API.
- **Client-side alternative (noted, not recommended for enforcement):** the model is open, so it could run in the browser (about 25 to 125 MB download) and send nothing. But a browser check can be bypassed, so it cannot be the safety layer; it could at most power an advisory "this may land hard" nudge. Keep server-side as the authority.

## 3. Decision table (draft)
| Matcher | Model | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nothing | low | nothing |
| nothing | high | **offer a card** or soften the reply only; no boundary |
| match, cue present | low | offer a card; level 1 (acknowledge) at most |
| match | high | level 1 or 2 per the ladder |
| harm-to-others / crisis rule | any | existing stop (unchanged, comes first) |
| reporting, quoting, grief frame | any | nothing (the frame rule wins unless the harm rule fires) |
Level 3 (ending) stays out until the measured false-alarm rate allows it. Thresholds are set on our evaluation sets, not by feel, and the table itself is versioned.

## 4. How it improves without real-user text
**A fixed benchmark ladder** (all offline, counts only, nothing in the repository): HateCheck (per functionality), ETHOS, MetaHate's non-hateful half (false alarms), ToxiGen's own test data, and **our hand-written scenes** (must-not-count and multi-turn, written with a counselor and an advocate; these are the ones that matter most). Every checker version (matcher list version, model name and hash, thresholds) gets a row of results in a versioned table in `docs/`, so a change is a visible before and after, and a worse number blocks the change.

**Synthetic data for our own domain.** Public sets are social-media comments; our traffic is reflective, often angry or grieving text addressed to a coach. We write **scene templates** (a person describing a quarrel, a quote of someone else's hateful remark, grief about a war, a venting rant with no group target) and expand them with an AI model plus **human review** (a counselor and an advocate read a sample). No user text goes into the generator, so no user data is involved. The synthetic set is for **measuring** first.

**Calibration before any tuning.** Choosing thresholds and combining the two signals per category is the safest improvement: no model changes. Next, if needed, a **small tuning step on public and synthetic data only** (a lighter head over the frozen model). **Decision for the owner: "training is not offered" and "conversations are never used to train" are about people's conversations and stay absolutely true; this is tuning on public and synthetic data. Do you accept that, with the Terms saying exactly that?** If not, we stay at calibration and pick better off-the-shelf models.

**Shadow mode on real traffic, with no text kept.** Before the model is allowed to act, it runs silently on live messages, and the server keeps only **bucketed counters per day**: how many messages the matcher flagged, how many the model scored in each of a few score bands, and how many of each combination. That tells us the real-world flag rate and how often the two agree **without retaining a single message or score**. It is the same kind of aggregate we already keep for daily totals. (Counters this coarse cannot identify anyone; the Privacy text names them.)

**A way for people to tell us it was wrong, on their terms.** A button on a card or a softened reply: "This was a mistake." By default it only ticks an aggregate counter for the rule name (no text, no identifier). Separately, and only if the person chooses, they can attach that one message through the existing consent flow (a signed consent receipt, a person reads it, they can take it back). That gives us real false-alarm cases from people who decided to share them, without collecting by default.

## 5. What is kept, and what people can control
- **Kept, always:** daily aggregate counters (flag counts by rule name and score band, model-unavailable count, mistake-button counts). No text, no scores per message, no identifier, nothing linked to a connection.
- **Never kept:** the message, the model's score for it, which words matched (already the rule for cards).
- **In the browser, for the visit:** the ladder's state, cleared by closing the tab, Forget me, Emergency Exit.
- **People can:** read how each checker works and what it can do (a "How the checks work" page); see **which checker acted and why** in the reply notice and the signed transcript (rule name and checker version, not a score); say "this was a mistake" without consequence; share an example by choice; erase everything held on their device.
- **People cannot** turn the checks off. The text says why: they are the only thing between a person and abuse or a call to harm, they run only on the latest message, they keep nothing, they can be wrong, and a wrong flag changes only the tone of one reply (and, at most, offers a pause), never anything about the person.
- **Signed transcripts** record the checker version (matcher list version and model name and hash) so a disputed flag can be tied to what produced it.

The wording goes in a draft first and ships only when the feature does (as with the learning cards).

## 8b. Owner's decisions, 2026-11-12
1. **Calibration plus light tuning on public and synthetic data only: accepted.** Never user data. The Terms must say exactly that when the model ships (conversations are never used to train; the checker may be tuned on public and synthetic text).
2. **Bucketed daily shadow counters: accepted.**
3. **Fail open to the matcher: accepted.**
4. **A separate `screen` function with the full model bundled inside it** (revised the same day: first bundle-in-relay, then, after comparing the two, the separate function). The relay calls `screen` and Venice at the same time; if `screen` is slow, down or crashes, the relay carries on with the matcher alone (fail open). Why: the model is the part most likely to misbehave (memory, cold start), and this way it can never take the coach down; it gets its own size limit (so the full model fits more easily), it can be updated or slimmed without touching the relay, and the relay's cold start stays short. Costs: one extra internal hop (tens of milliseconds), a second invocation per message, and one thing to protect: **`screen` is only callable by the relay** (a signed internal call or shared secret, no CORS, no public use as a classifier), and the text only ever moves between our own functions. **First build step:** a size and cold-start test with the chosen model (about 250 MB unzipped limit (verify)); if even the separate function is too big, slim the model, then move it to Blobs.
5. **"This was a mistake" button with the optional consented example: yes.**
6. **Synthetic scenes: the owner reviews them.** Beyond the owner, **contributors are invited to curate and validate** (a counselor, an advocate, and others from the community). That needs, before anyone is invited: (a) the contributor agreement (`docs/drafts/contributor-agreement-draft.md`) extended to cover this work; (b) **no user text anywhere in it**, only synthetic and public material; (c) a plain warning that the material includes hateful language and a way to stop at any time, because reading it is real work; (d) credit, if they want it, in the repository's credits; (e) the review protocol written down (who labels what, how disagreements are settled, how labels are versioned) so the benchmark does not drift. Contributors never get access to any user's message.

## 11. Model bake-off, first run (2026-11-12)
`scripts/bakeoff-models.py` (counts only; models in the research folder `models/`, downloaded with the owner's approval: Xenova `toxic-bert` `onnx/model_int8.onnx` with its tokenizer files; cardiffnlp `pytorch_model.bin` with its files). For each model and head it sets the threshold so that **at most 2% of the 1,730 ordinary cases** (HateCheck non-hateful and ETHOS non-hate together) are flagged, then reports the catch rate by HateCheck functionality and ETHOS label, what the model catches that the list and its gentle tiers do not, and the cost. Full output (no text): `results/public/bakeoff-2026-11-12.txt` in the research folder.
| Model (head) | file | warm latency | HateCheck hateful caught | ETHOS hate caught | ETHOS violence | caught only by the model (HateCheck / ETHOS) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| list and gentle tiers alone | private | microseconds | 5.8% | 13.6% | 17/136 | n/a |
| toxic-bert int8, `toxic` | 110 MB | 16 ms | 2.6% | 15.9% | 35/136 | 66 / 53 |
| toxic-bert int8, `identity_hate` | same | same | 11.3% | 14.8% | 28/136 | 260 / 42 |
| toxic-bert int8, `threat` | same | same | 4.6% | 6.0% | 25/136 | 106 / 20 (direct threats 54%) |
| **cardiffnlp hate, fp32** | 499 MB | 18 ms | **30.9%** | **30.5%** | **56/136** | **731 / 103** |
| cardiffnlp hate, int8 (torch dynamic) | 242 MB | 17 ms | 26.2% | 26.1% | 46/136 | 617 / 87 |
**Reading it:**
- **cardiffnlp (hate) is the clear leader** at the same 2% false-alarm rate: about 30% of both sets caught against 5.8% and 13.6% for the list, and it catches what the list cannot: ETHOS gender 21 of 79 (list 1), national origin 27 of 68 (list 6), religion 34 of 75 (list 14), sexual orientation 21 of 73 (list 13), violence 56 of 136 (list 17). **Together with the list and tiers, ETHOS hate reached rises from 13.6% to about 37%** (59 + 103 of 433).
- **toxic-bert measures toxicity, not hate**, as expected: its `toxic` and `insult` heads find profanity (33% of HateCheck profanity) and little else; its `threat` head is the best direct-threat reader (54% of `threat_dir`). It could serve the judgment tier or threats, not the hate signal.
- **Where cardiffnlp fails:** the slur lookalikes (27% of `slur_homonym` flagged), reclaimed slurs (5%), negation (4%), and the quote and denounce frames are fine (2% and 0%). Those are the context cases the frame rules and the list's `model` terms handle; **the model must not act alone on them**.
- **Size:** fp32 is 499 MB, over the function limit. torch's dynamic int8 gives 242 MB with a loss of about 5 points (30.9% to 26.2%); an ONNX int8 export (embeddings quantized too) should be near 125 MB (an estimate, not yet done), which fits with room. Latency here is 17 to 18 ms warm on this Mac; the function's CPU will be slower (not measured).
**Caveats, honestly:**
1. **The threshold is chosen on the same ordinary cases it is then measured on**, so the false-alarm rates are slightly optimistic. A held-out set (MetaHate's non-hate half and our hand-written scenes) is needed before a threshold is fixed.
2. **cardiffnlp was trained on 13 hate datasets** (its card lists them: HatEval, Measuring Hate Speech, HateXplain, OLID, Davidson and others; **ETHOS and HateCheck are not among them**, so these tests are fair). **MetaHate overlaps those datasets (it is built from many of them), so MetaHate would flatter this model**; use it for the list and for toxic-bert, and treat it as contaminated for cardiffnlp.
3. Peak memory is cumulative across models in one process, so the figures printed are not per model; measure it in the function.
4. Single-message latency on a Mac, not on the host; the ONNX int8 export and the cold start in the function are the next measurements.
5. Not yet measured: the hand-written scenes against the models (self-judgment, wishes, denial and the history patterns), the multi-turn case, and bias checks (does it flag identity terms alone, dialect, reclaimed use; `ident_neutral` and `ident_pos` are 0% flagged, a good sign; `slur_homonym` and the AAE and dialect cases are the ones to test).
**Recommendation:** adopt **cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-hate-latest** (CC-BY-4.0; credit needed) as the screening model, as an ONNX int8 export; keep **toxic-bert's `threat` head** as an optional second reader for threats only; hold ToxiGen until its licence is stated (and it is RoBERTa-large). Next: the ONNX int8 export and its size and accuracy; the held-out and bias tests; then the `screen` function build with shadow mode.

## 12. The ONNX int8 export (2026-11-12)
`scripts/export-onnx.py` exports `cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-hate-latest` to ONNX (opset 17), then quantizes it with onnxruntime's dynamic int8 quantization (files in the research folder `models/cardiffnlp-onnx/`; a venv at `Nenya Research/tool/pyvenv` holds the one added package, `onnx`).
- **Size:** fp32 ONNX 498 MB; **int8 125 MB** (the estimate held). The embedding table is quantized too by default; asking for it explicitly changed nothing.
- **Fidelity:** on six invented sentences the int8 scores differ from torch fp32 by at most 0.002.
- **Bake-off at the same 2% false-alarm rate (the exported int8 file, scored alone):** HateCheck hateful caught **29.0%** (fp32 30.9%), ETHOS **28.9%** (30.5%); ETHOS violence 52 of 136 (list and tiers 17); gender 19 of 79, national origin 26 of 68, religion 33 of 75, sexual orientation 17 of 73; only the model catches 685 HateCheck and 96 ETHOS hateful cases. The loss from quantizing is under two points. `slur_homonym` is still flagged 27% (a context case for the list's `model` terms and the frame rules, not for the model alone).
- **Cost (this Mac, one process):** load 0.2 s, **warm latency median 9 ms (p95 33 ms)**, peak process memory 880 MB (this includes Python, the datasets and the tokenizer; the figure in the function will differ and must be measured there).
- **Function fit:** 125 MB model plus the tokenizer files (about 3 MB) plus the ONNX runtime for Node: well under about 250 MB unzipped if only the Linux build of the runtime is bundled (to verify when the function is built).
- **Next:** (1) held-out and bias tests (the hand-written scenes against the model; dialect, identity terms alone and reclaimed use; thresholds chosen on one half and checked on the other); (2) the `screen` function with the model, the signed internal call and shadow mode, off in production; (3) `ATTRIBUTIONS.md` credit for the model (CC-BY-4.0: the authors, the paper, the licence, and that we quantized it).

## 13. Held-out and bias checks on the int8 model (2026-11-12)
`scripts/bakeoff-heldout-bias.py`; invented neutral sentences in `src/lib/biasScenes.json` (161: five sentence types, "I am X", "my friend is X", "X are people like everyone else", a report of unfair treatment, pride, across 27 identity groups, plus dialect, reclaimed-use and mention sentences). Output: `results/public/bakeoff-heldout-bias-2026-11-12.txt` in the research folder.
**Held-out (200 random splits of the 1,730 ordinary cases; the threshold set on one half to flag 2%, checked on the other):** the false-alarm rate on the unseen half is **median 2.0%, 5th to 95th percentile 1.2% to 3.2%, worst 4.2%**, so the threshold generalizes; the share of the 2,996 hateful cases caught is median **29.0%** (range 24.2% to 35.4%); the threshold is stable (0.991 to 0.994, median 0.993). The trade-off: 1% false alarms catches 19.3%, 2% catches 29.0%, 5% catches 42.1%. **The 2% bar holds with an honest margin of error of about one point either way.**
**Bias on neutral sentences at the chosen threshold (0.993): 0 of 135 flagged across all 27 identity groups**, and no sentence type is flagged ("self", "friend", "plural", "report", "pride" all 0 of 27). Scores are tiny for every group (median group mean 0.002); the highest is "a man" (mean 0.056, max 0.234, well under the threshold), then gay (0.009) and lesbian (0.006); a report of unfair treatment scores higher than the others (mean 0.013), as expected. **Dialect: 0 of 8 African American Vernacular English sentences flagged (mean 0.002, against 0.001 for their standard paraphrases), 0 of 3 second-language sentences, 0 of 1 Scots; reclaimed self-description 0 of 4; mention of a slur in teaching 0 of 2.**
**What this does not show:** the sets are small and invented (five sentences per group; eight for dialect), neutral only; they would not reveal a model that flags hateful text about one group more than another, or one that misses hate in dialect. Larger and harder bias sets need either published ones (HateCheck's target-group split is a start; ETHOS's labels) or the hand-written scenes written with the reviewers. A per-group look at HateCheck's hateful cases by target group (women, trans, gay, Black, disabled, Muslim, immigrant) is the next bias measure, with the same threshold.
**Credit:** the model is credited in `ATTRIBUTIONS.md` (CC-BY-4.0: the authors, the paper, the licence, the changes: ONNX conversion and int8 quantization, no retraining; no endorsement).

## 14. The `screen` function, built (2026-11-12; off in production, untested on Netlify)
**Pieces:** `netlify/functions/screen.mts` (path `/api/screen`; scores one message and answers a band, never the score or the text; keeps and logs nothing about it), `src/lib/screenModel.ts` (the ONNX int8 model through `onnxruntime-web`, WebAssembly, so no native binary to ship, and the pure-JavaScript `@huggingface/tokenizers`; bands from the bake-off: "high" above 0.993, the 2% line, "mid" above 0.985, the 5% line), `src/lib/screenAuth.ts` (the relay's signed call: the time and an HMAC-SHA256 of the time and the body made with `COACH_SCREEN_SECRET`, valid thirty seconds, bound to its body; without the secret the endpoint answers 404), `src/lib/screenClient.ts` (the relay's side: a 400 ms budget, never throws, any failure is "unavailable"), `scripts/upload-screen-model.mts` (puts the 125 MB int8 file and the tokenizer files in the private store, keys `screen/...`), two packages added (`onnxruntime-web` pinned at 1.22.0, `@huggingface/tokenizers`), and `netlify.toml` marks both as external modules for the function so they ship whole.
**Shadow mode in the relay (`COACH_SCREEN=shadow` plus `COACH_SCREEN_SECRET`; both unset by default, so nothing changes):** after the safety checks and only when no flag applies, the latest message goes to our own screening function while the model answers; only counters are written: `coach.screen.band.high|mid|low`, `coach.screen.vs-list.<band>.list|nolist` (did the phrase matcher also act), and `coach.screen.unavailable` (a slow or failed call, fail open). The reply never depends on it. The model never acts yet: the decision table (section 3) is not wired, by design, until the shadow counts and a held-out check say it should be.
**Measured here:** the model scores correctly through this code path (a kind sentence about 0.00, a call for killing about 1.00, matching the Python run); about 83 ms per message warm and 0.4 s to load from local disk with WebAssembly on one thread (the Python runtime was 9 ms; the budget is 400 ms and the call runs beside the model's own, which takes seconds); peak process memory about 800 MB with the model loaded. **Tests (25):** signing and verification, bands, the function's responses (a signed call, an unsigned one, a bad method, empty text, no secret, a failing model), the client's budget and failure handling, the shadow counters through the coach function (off by default, low and high, down and slow, never sent for a message that stops for safety, no text in the stored counts), and the real model when its files are present.
**Not verified (do before switching it on, on staging first):** that Netlify packages `onnxruntime-web` with its `.wasm` file under the `external_node_modules` setting (the first deploy's log and a call will show it); the function's real memory use against its limit (about 800 MB here is close to a 1 GB default: if it is too tight the options are a slimmer runtime, a smaller model, or a higher memory setting); the cold start: the 125 MB download from the private store plus load, several seconds; the first request after a cold start will be "unavailable" and later ones fast; and the rate limit (the function has none of its own; the signature stops outside callers cheaply). **Steps for the owner, in order:** set `COACH_SCREEN_SECRET` (the same random value for the whole site) on staging; run `scripts/upload-screen-model.mts` with the site id and token for staging's store; deploy; set `COACH_SCREEN=shadow`; read the counters in the admin view; production only after staging is understood.

## 15. The message form check (2026-11-12)
A message with no words to read (under two words or five letters, or almost all keyboard mashing, in the Latin alphabet; other scripts only the length) is not sent on and **uses no turn**: the page shows a gentle line and keeps the draft, and the server (`blocked: 'form'`, counted as `coach.turn.blocked.form.short|unclear`) is the one that counts. **Never when a safety flag applies** (the page and the server check the flags first, so a short or oddly spelled crisis message goes through its own checks); `kms` was added as a soften phrase for that reason. `COACH_FORM_CHECK=off` turns it off (the tests run with it off, except `tests/form-gate-function.test.mts`). ## 16. Per-target-group measure (2026-11-13)
`scripts/bakeoff-target-groups.py`: HateCheck labels each case with the group it is about; the int8 model at the chosen threshold (0.994, the 2% line), the list and its gentle tiers, counts only (`results/public/bakeoff-target-groups-2026-11-13.txt`).

By kind of hate: threats 89.7% (Muslims) against 15.4% (disabled); dehumanizing comparisons 75% (Muslims) and 70% (women) against 10% (disabled, trans); **slurs: 0% by the model and 25% by the list for trans people, 12.5% for women** (the list has almost no slurs about women, and its trans slurs are few). **Non-hateful cases flagged (model):** low everywhere (0% to 2.7%) except **gay people 6.2% [3-11]** (reclaimed in-group words such as "queer" and "dyke" in neutral or proud statements; the list sends these to the model, the model sometimes flags them). Neutral and positive identity statements are flagged 0% for every group, by both, so naming a group never triggers anything. The list's apparent 16% to 20% for Black and gay people in the non-hateful table is **deferral to the model** (reclaimed spellings), not card offers (card offers on non-hateful HateCheck are 3 of 1,165 overall).
**What to do about it (owner's decisions):**
1. **Coverage work first**, as already planned: reviewer-sourced slurs and dehumanizing terms for women, trans people and disabled people (Wiktionary had no category for them). That raises the list's share, which is flat today.
2. **A second model reading for these groups.** Candidates to test per group before choosing: toxic-bert's `identity_hate` head (it is trained on a different data source), or a per-group threshold (more complex, and it treats groups differently on purpose, so it needs the owner's and the reviewers' view).
3. **Say it plainly on the "How the checks work" page and in the Terms:** the checks are uneven across groups today, are being improved, and the coach's stance (the steering note) does not depend on the checks alone.
4. **Track it:** this script's table becomes a standing row in the evaluation record, re-run with every model or list change; a gap that widens blocks the change.
**Limits:** HateCheck is synthetic and in English; each group has about 360 hateful cases (about 16 to 24 for a given kind), so the interval around a single cell is wide (the table prints them); the groups are the seven HateCheck covers, not every group our rules cover.

## 17. Second model reading, tested (2026-11-13)
`scripts/bakeoff-second-reading.py` (counts only; `results/public/bakeoff-second-reading-2026-11-13.txt`): toxic-bert's heads (int8 ONNX) alone and combined with the hate model at the **same total false-alarm budget** (2%).
- **toxic-bert `identity_hate` alone (2% budget): 11.6% of HateCheck hate, 13.9% of ETHOS**; by group: Black 27%, gay 25%, Muslims 12%, trans 11%, disabled 2%, immigrants 1%, **women 0%**. It is strongest where the hate model is weakest only for Black and gay people; for trans and disabled people it is no better, and for women and immigrants far worse.
- **Combined with the hate model at the same budget (1% OR 1%, 1.5% OR 0.5%, 1.2% OR 0.8%, and with the `threat` head): overall catch 24.5% to 27.4% against 27.9% for the hate model alone, so no gain; the lowest group stays disabled people at 8% to 11% (10% alone), trans people at 11% to 13%.** The spread across groups does not close (31 to 37 points against 39).
- **Conclusion: a second general model reading does not close the gap.** The two models learned from similar kinds of data, and neither learned much about trans or disabled people. The `insult`, `threat` and `toxic` heads catch 4% to 6% of hate (they measure rudeness, not hate).
**What would:** (1) **coverage work on the list** (terms for women, trans and disabled people, already planned: this is the part that is ours to add and review, and it is flat today); (2) **a model trained on data that includes those groups.** ToxiGen's data was made for exactly this (its groups include women, LGBTQ, and physical and mental disability); its models are `tomh/toxigen_roberta` (large, no licence stated) and `tomh/toxigen_hatebert` (BERT-base, about 110 MB at int8; licence not checked). **Next step: read ToxiGen's licence and the hatebert model's card, then run the same per-group table on it (a download needs the owner's say-so).** (3) Until then, say the unevenness plainly (section 16, item 3).
